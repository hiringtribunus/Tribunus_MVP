# Tribunus — Market Validation & Startup Viability

*A research-analyst assessment of whether Tribunus (Wedge B: pre-permitting approval-risk intelligence for large BC developers) is a real startup worth pursuing. Every material claim is triangulated across at least two independent source types — hard data (industry bodies, CMHC, consultancies) and practitioner/market reality (developer accounts, competitor funding, adoption studies). Sources listed at the end.*

**Date:** August 2026 · **Verdict confidence:** Medium-high on the problem; medium on the business, gated by adoption and market size.

---

## Bottom line up front

**The pain is real, severe, expensive, and unusually well-timed — that part is not in doubt.** The open question is not *"is there a problem"* (there emphatically is) but *"will a conservative, concentrated set of developers adopt a software tool in place of, or alongside, the trusted human consultants they use today"* — and *"is the BC market big enough to matter."* Those are execution and market-size questions, which is exactly what a design-partner pilot is designed to answer. My read: **worth pursuing to a pilot**, with eyes open about the two real risks. The evidence supports a genuine painkiller in a fundable category with a rare regulatory tailwind; it does not yet prove venture-scale, which hinges on expanding beyond BC.

Scorecard, evidence-weighted:

| Dimension | Grade | One-line basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the pain real & acute? | **A** | 13–14 month avg BC approvals; Vancouver longest in Canada; delay directly linked to cost. |
| Is there budget / willingness to pay? | **B+** | Soft costs are 15–30% of project budget; developers already pay consultants/expediters to de-risk. |

| Execution risk (adoption/data/trust) | **C** | Real estate is conservative and slow to adopt; municipal data is messy; high-stakes trust barrier. |

## 1. The pain is real and quantified (highest-confidence finding)

Multiple independent authorities converge on the same picture, which is what makes this finding solid rather than anecdotal:

- **CHBA BC's Municipal Benchmarking Study (3rd ed., 2024, released March 2025):** rezoning and development-permit approvals in BC take **13–14 months on average**, and two of BC's largest municipalities rank in the national bottom ten for process.
- **CMHC's Municipal Land Use and Regulation Survey:** approval averages roughly **a full year** for multi-unit housing nationally, and **Vancouver and Toronto show the longest delays in Canada.** CMHC's own research paper is titled *"Approval Delays Linked with Lower Housing Affordability."*
- **Altus Group (for CHBA):** in the comparable Toronto market, approval time rose from **21 months (2020) to 32 months (2022)** — the trend is worsening, not improving.
- **The cost of that delay is direct and large:** reported BC cases include a project whose cost rose **~$300,000 during a 13-month approval wait**, and developers describe cancelling or shelving projects when timelines and uncertainty stack up. Municipal fees on new homes vary **more than tenfold** between cities (single-family charges from under $10k to nearly $200k) — the exact fee-surprise risk Tribunus targets.
- **Practitioner voice confirms the *uncertainty*, not just the delay:** developers repeatedly cite *"lack of communication about what stage an application is at"* and *"considerable frustrations and delays,"* especially for larger multi-family projects — i.e. they are flying blind on risk and timing, which is the emotional core of Wedge B.

**Triangulation verdict:** industry association + federal agency + independent consultancy + on-record developer accounts all agree. The pain is not manufactured. This is a must-have-shaped problem.

## 2. Budget exists — but today it's spent on people

Developers already pay substantially to de-risk approvals, which means the money is in the system:

- **Soft costs run 15–30% of the total project budget**, concentrated in the predevelopment/entitlement phase (permits, legal, design, consultants). On a large developer's project that is millions of dollars, and approval risk sits squarely inside it.
- Direct line items: **planning consultants $5–15k+** (far more on complex files), **rezoning all-in from a few thousand to well over $100k**, Vancouver rezoning application fees **$12k–$50k+**, permit expediters **$2k–$15k+** on commercial work.

The caveat that matters: this budget currently flows to **trusted humans — land-use planning consultants** who "manage the technical and strategic aspects of obtaining rezoning and entitlements on behalf of the developer." That relationship is Tribunus's true incumbent (see §5). Willingness to pay is proven; *willingness to redirect that spend to software* is the thing to test.

## 3. Timing is genuinely exceptional (the strongest strategic argument)

This is the rare case where "why now" is not hand-waving:

- **BC rewrote the rules and the rewrite is contested and inconsistent.** Bill 44 (SSMUH), Bill 47 (TOAs), Bill 46 (ACCs) landed in 2023; one year in, implementation "varied widely," some municipalities kept barriers on paper vs. practice, and the Province had to pass **Bill 25 (2025)** to close loopholes — while municipalities mounted **judicial-review challenges**. The result: the rules that govern a developer's most expensive decisions are *changing, jurisdiction-specific, and litigated in real time.* No human can hold that current in their head across Vancouver + Coquitlam + neighbours — which is precisely the "cited, dated, current-vs-superseded" value Tribunus offers.
- **The activity is real:** Vancouver issued **643 multiplex permits in 2025 (+46% YoY, the first year multiplex crossed single-family)**, with 400+ multiplex applications across Metro Vancouver — new development types under new, unsettled rules.
- **AI capability crossed the threshold** to actually read and cite municipal corpora, and **governments themselves are validating the direction** (BC's LGDAP funding digital-permitting projects; Kelowna's $350k Microsoft AI chatbot answering zoning/OCP questions). That government digitization is a double-edged signal — it eases *their* intake but doesn't de-risk the *developer's* decision, so the wedge survives.

**Triangulation verdict:** regulatory chaos (legislation + enforcement + litigation) and AI readiness and government co-signal all point to a now-or-rarely window.

## 4. Market size — the honest weak spot

- The buyer set is **narrow and concentrated.** UDI Pacific has 850+ *corporate members* but that spans the whole ecosystem (planners, lawyers, engineers, lenders); the actual *developer* buyer count is a subset, and BC's market is characterized as "a relatively small group of developers responsible for the bulk" of projects. Your beachhead (large Metro Vancouver developers) is realistically **dozens to ~100 high-value accounts.**
- That cuts both ways: it's a **small TAM** for a BC-only product, but it's also **high-ACV and reachable** — you can name every logo, and enterprise pricing against a multi-million-dollar soft-cost line is defensible. A concentrated market is easier to sell into and easier to cap out of.
- **Venture-scale therefore depends on geographic expansion.** The modular, jurisdiction-scoped architecture (per tech_stack.md) is explicitly built for this — add a municipality as a data slice. The BC pilot is the proof; other Canadian metros with the same delay pathology (Toronto especially) are the scale story. As a BC-only business it's a viable, profitable niche; as a venture bet it needs the expansion thesis to be credible.

## 5. Competition — funded category, local white space, human incumbent

- **The category is real and VC-backed**, which de-risks "is this fundable": PropTech funding rebounded to **$2.2B in 2024** (from $717M in 2023), and permitting/zoning specifically is hot — **PermitFlow ($91M total, Kleiner Perkins-led Series A), GreenLite ($49.5M Series B, Insight Partners, 2025), Pulley,** and **Zoneomics' "Bassett" (2025) — an AI zoning/permitting OS that reasons over ordinances and cites authoritative sources**, which is conceptually the closest thing to Tribunus.
- **But those winners are US and jurisdiction-specific.** Zoning/permitting intelligence is inherently local; US zoning ≠ BC entitlement, and none of these operate in the BC regulatory reality. Several (Gridics, Symbium) even sell primarily to *municipalities* (govtech), not developers. **No direct BC developer-side approval-risk-intelligence competitor surfaced.** That's your opening — and the reason a US player can't trivially parachute in is the same reason you have to go deep: local depth is the moat.
- **The real incumbent is not software — it's the trusted planning consultant.** Developers de-risk approvals today by hiring people they trust. Tribunus's smartest posture is probably **"augment, don't only replace"**: a tool that makes the developer (and even their consultant) faster and more certain, and potentially a *channel* through consultants, rather than a frontal assault on a relationship-driven habit.

## 6. What could kill it (execution risks, evidence-based)

- **Adoption conservatism is the #1 documented proptech killer.** Real estate is "conservative, relationship-driven, risk-averse," with long sales cycles, procurement inertia, and low tolerance for system failure; **67% of proptech implementations reportedly fail to deliver expected ROI**, and investors' most common critique is "solution-first thinking" — impressive tech aimed at a nice-to-have. Tribunus must be a **painkiller, not a vitamin**, and must survive enterprise sales cycles.
- **Municipal data is messy and often manual.** The product's credibility depends on current, correct, cited municipal data; in the pilot this is hand-curated (acknowledged in the plan). Keeping it fresh and right across changing bylaws is an operating cost and a failure mode — one wrong, confidently-stated fact damages trust disproportionately in a *trust* product.
- **Trust and liability on high-stakes decisions.** Developers won't bet millions on an AI assertion unless provenance is airtight. This is why the **citation backbone + human-in-the-loop is not optional** — it's the core of viability, not a feature.
- **Market ceiling** (see §4) and **regulatory whiplash cutting the other way** — if the Province ever *simplifies and stabilizes* the rules, some urgency fades (though complexity rarely truly recedes).

---

## 7. Verdict & what would most de-risk it

**Is this a good startup?** On the evidence: **yes as a real, painful, well-timed problem with existing budget and a local white space — with two honest asterisks:** adoption behaviour and BC market size. It is a *strong wedge and a viable business*; whether it's a *venture-scale rocket* depends on nailing the beachhead and then generalizing beyond BC. The single best thing about the opportunity is timing — the Bill 44/46/47/25 upheaval is a genuine, possibly narrow, window where the rules are too fluid to track by hand.

**The predictors of success to watch — and what a design-partner pilot should explicitly test:**

1. **Must-have vs. nice-to-have.** Does the developer change a real decision because of a Tribunus finding (kill a deal, change tenure, re-sequence a pathway, avoid a fee surprise)? If yes → painkiller. Instrument this from day one — it's also why the **feedback/outcome loop belongs in the MVP**.
2. **Trust conversion.** Do they believe the cited findings enough to act? Watch whether the citation/provenance UX actually earns that trust.
3. **Consultant dynamic.** Do planning consultants treat it as a threat or reach for it as leverage? A consultant channel could crack the adoption problem the whole industry struggles with.
4. **Willingness to redirect spend.** Will a developer pay software pricing against a budget currently going to people? Even one paid pilot answers more than any survey.
5. **A credible path to metro #2.** Prove the jurisdiction-add is weeks, not a rewrite — that's the difference between a nice BC business and a venture story.

**Recommended posture:** proceed to a design partner, but frame the pilot as a *falsifiable test of adoption*, not a demo — pick one large Metro Vancouver developer with active Vancouver/Coquitlam files, be a painkiller on the pre-permitting risk decision, and measure whether findings change behaviour. The problem is proven; the pilot is to prove the *business*.
